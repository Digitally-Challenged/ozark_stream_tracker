Every year has a high-water mark. How big were they?

Two record-class floods in twenty-four years — and eight quiet ones.

High confidence · USGS annual peaks, all approved

2008 and 2025 both topped 22 ft. Between them sit a lot of ordinary years — a third of them never reached the 10-ft minor-flood line. That mix, not a steady climb, is what the record shows.

How often does the river hit each flood stage?

10 ft most years. 16 ft about every four. 23 ft is a once-in-a-generation event.

Moderate confidence · fitted on 24 years; the rare end is wide

Read these as odds, not a schedule: a 1-in-4½-year flood has roughly a 20 % chance in any given year, and two can land back to back (2008 did it twice in a month).

Is the summer low water drying up?

Low water rises and falls with the previous year's rain — no hidden decline.

High confidence · 45 years at the spring, no gaps

Mammoth Spring is the river's base flow, and its lowest week each year tracks the rain that fell in the twelve months before it. After removing that rain signal, what's left drifts by about −0.2 % a year — a range that comfortably includes zero.

In numbers: residual trend −0.0022 log-cfs/yr, 95 % CI −0.0050 to +0.0005, n = 42. Hardy's own record (2002+) tells the same story once rain is accounted for.

Why does the river look lower at the same flow?

The gauge's channel has dropped about 4 inches since 2008. The water hasn't.

High confidence · 667,000 paired stage–flow readings

At a steady 1,000 cfs the gauge read 3.94 ft in 2008 and 3.52 ft in 2025 — a drop of 0.019 ft a year (95 % CI −0.021 to −0.016). That is the riverbed at the gauge changing shape, not less water. Anyone judging the river by stage alone will think it's lower than it is.

What does a foot on the gauge mean in water?

The whole paddling range fits inside one foot of stage. Floods take the other twenty.

High confidence · 667,000 paired stage–flow readings, r = 0.97

Median flow (690 cfs) sits at about 3.2 ft; the middle half of all days spans just 3.0–3.7 ft. Stage is a compressed ruler at low water and a stretched one in flood — and because of the channel drop above, the same gauge reading now carries 8–18 % more water than it did in 2008. Old stage-based rules of thumb for runnability underestimate today's flow.

Do big floods "flush out" the aquifer and leave the spring low afterwards?

The opposite: the six months after a major flood run higher than comparable years.

Moderate confidence · only six major floods to compare; descriptive, not causal

Every one of the six comparisons came out higher. The likeliest reading is simple: the storms that make major floods also soak the recharge country, and the spring pays that back for months.

Is it raining harder over the spring's watershed?

Yes — about 2.4 inches more per decade, mostly as more wet days, not just bigger storms.

Moderate confidence · gridded rain data; the single weather station can't confirm it

Days with an inch or more are up by about 1.4 per decade; the wettest day of the year is up about a quarter inch. The one thing not rising is winter recharge rain, which is why the spring's base flow is steady rather than climbing.

How long after it rains does the spring respond?

About a month. The signal is mostly gone by nine.

High confidence · 545 months of paired data

A wet month shows up in the spring the following month and fades over most of a year. That is the memory of the aquifer — and the reason a dry winter shows up as a low August.

When do the big floods come — and is that changing?

Late winter to early spring, same as it was in the 1940s.

High confidence · 89 years of peaks downstream at Imboden

Decades wander between mid-January and early April with no direction to the wander. Recession behaviour is just as steady: after a flood, Hardy drains at the same rate it did in 2008 (median 14 days per e-fold, no trend), and the spring at the same slow rate (median 188 days).

What would change these answers

  • Twenty-four years is short for floods. The 16-ft and 23-ft return periods rest on 24 Hardy peaks. The 1982 record crest is real but has no flow measurement, so it isn't in the fit. Ranges are wide on purpose.
  • The gauge's own history is missing. The USGS keeps records of when and how it re-calibrated the Hardy gauge. Until those are obtained, the channel-drop finding comes from the readings alone.
  • The rain trend is a model product. PRISM blends stations into a grid. The West Plains station itself has too many missing years to confirm or deny the trend on its own.
  • Six floods make a pattern, not a proof. The post-flood finding is a comparison, not an experiment.
  • The recharge area is approximated as a 30-km circle around West Plains; the dye-traced boundary would be better.
