Spring River at Hardy, Arkansas · 1948 – 2026 · final report

Spring River in Plain Terms

What seventy-eight years of rain gauges and forty-five years of river gauges say about a spring-fed Ozark river.

Gauges Mammoth Spring (the source), Hardy (the town), Imboden (downstream) Rain NOAA AORC hourly grid over the traced recharge area; West Plains gauge 1948–2026 Last data discharge 24 Aug 2026 · rain 31 Dec 2025

The Spring River does not behave like most rivers. Its base flow — the water that is there when it has not rained — comes out of the ground at Mammoth Spring, one of the largest springs in the Ozarks, fed by an underground drainage area that straddles the Missouri line. On top of that steady floor sit short, violent floods that arrive with heavy rain and leave within days.

People who live on, paddle, or fish the river have asked the same questions for years: is the floor sinking? Are floods getting bigger or more frequent? Is it raining more? Does a big flood wash out the spring's supply? This page answers each one from the numbers and says how sure we are.

Where the water comes from

The ground that feeds the spring

Rain that feeds Mammoth Spring does not fall on the river. It falls on a plateau of fractured limestone to the north — around West Plains, Missouri, and the country south-east of it toward Alton and Thayer — sinks, and travels underground to the spring. The Missouri Geological Survey has traced that recharge area with dye: about 349 square miles, with the West Plains gauges just inside its north-west edge.

Rainfall over that area is taken from NOAA's AORC analysis, a one-kilometre hourly grid that blends radar (from 2002), rain gauges, and weather-model output, averaged over the traced boundary; a second gridded product (PRISM) over the same boundary is used as a cross-check, and the West Plains gauges themselves as the long record.

The source

The spring has its own gauge

Mammoth Spring rises just below the Missouri line, and the U.S. Geological Survey has measured its outflow every day since 25 February 1981 (station 07069190). In forty-five years the record has not missed a single day. It is the most direct measurement there is of the aquifer that feeds this river, and it is why the questions on this page can be answered from 1981 rather than from 2002, when the Hardy gauge began.

A spring does not behave like a river. Its flow moves inside a narrow band — half of all days fall between about 290 and 410 cubic feet per second — and its whole 45-year range, from 173 cfs in November 2001 to 689 cfs in April 1991, is a factor of four. Hardy's range is more than two hundred. What the spring does do is remember: its output this month reflects the rain of the last year, with an echo of the year before.

Base flow

Is the spring's floor sinking? no

Each year we take the driest seven consecutive days at Mammoth Spring and ask what explains them. The answer is last year's rain. The lowest week tracks how much fell over the twelve months before it, with a one-year echo; that rain explains 58 % of the year-to-year swing, and once it is removed the leftover trend is −0.13 % a year with a range that comfortably includes zero (95 % interval −0.33 % to +0.12 %, 42 years).

At Hardy the floor is rising unsettled

Hardy's own record only starts in 2002. Its driest week has climbed from around 200 cfs to around 400 cfs over those 24 years, and that part is not in doubt (+2.3 % a year, interval +0.7 to +3.6). The question is why. Antecedent rain explains part of it. What is left over depends on which rainfall dataset you use:

  • On AORC, the primary dataset, the unexplained rise is +2.0 % a year and its interval (+0.9 to +2.9) does not include zero.
  • On PRISM over the same area, it is +1.0 % a year with an interval that touches zero (−0.06 to +1.9).

One dataset says "something beyond rain"; the other says "nothing beyond rain". With 24 years and a model that explains only 41 % of the variation, we report this as dataset-dependent rather than as a finding. Whatever it is, it is real water and not a gauge artefact — see "Could the gravel be fooling the gauge?" below — and it enters the river between the spring and the town.

Could the gravel be fooling the gauge? checked — no

A fair challenge to the rising floor at Hardy: the two great floods of 2008 and 2025 visibly rearranged the riverbed, and the channel through town is noticeably shallower and wider than it was. A gauge does not measure flow; it measures water level and converts it through a rating curve that hydrographers re-fit by wading out and measuring the flow directly. If the gravel moved and the rating did not keep up, the "rise" could be an accounting error.

The bed did move. Stage at a fixed 1,000 cfs fell 0.15 ft in 2008–2010 alone, and a 2016 visit is logged by the hydrographer as "scour control changed". But the hydrographers' 66 direct low-flow measurements since 2002 sit on the rating within about one percent in every era — before the 2008 flood (+0.8 %), after it (0.0 %), and after 2015 (+0.6 %). Only this year has the gap opened, to +3.7 % across four visits since the April 2025 flood: the gauge is currently reading slightly low, not high. The rating has tracked the channel; the reported low flows are real water.

So the gravel changed the stage but not the flow — and the extra water is real. Where it comes from is the open question: the low-flow gain between Mammoth Spring and Hardy stepped from about 40 cfs (2002–2007) to about 130 cfs (2009 on) and has stayed there, and there is no gauge on the tributaries in between to say which of them is contributing it.

The gauge

Why the stage readings look lower

Anyone watching the Hardy gauge has seen the late-summer stage sit lower each year — 3.35 feet in 2015, 3.0 in 2017, under 3 feet now. But stage is a ruler on a riverbed, and the riverbed moves. Comparing readings at the same flow, the stage at 1,000 cfs has fallen 0.019 ft a year (interval 0.016 to 0.021, 19 years) — about a quarter of a foot since 2012. The riffle that controls the water level at the gauge has lowered and widened; the water is not gone. The USGS's own direct measurements at the site confirm it (previous section): the rating was re-fitted as the bed changed, and the reported flows agree with the wading measurements to within about one percent.

Floods

How often, how big, and is it changing? no change

Hardy has only 24 annual peaks, so for trend we lean on Imboden, 30 miles downstream, which has 89 (1937–2025). Imboden's yearly peaks show no drift (+0.1 % a year, interval −0.3 to +0.9). Splitting the record before and after 2008 — the year of the biggest modern flood — gives ten-year flood estimates whose uncertainty ranges overlap. Nothing in the data says floods are becoming larger or more common.

The two biggest floods: March 2008 and April 2025

By discharge the April 2025 flood edged out March 2008 as the largest in the Hardy gauge's 24-year record — 83,700 cfs against 80,700, a difference of four percent — with a higher crest (22.82 ft vs 22.29). But 2008 moved more water: its ten days around the peak carried 247,000 acre-feet to 2025's 229,000, because 2008 came as a broad, two-crested event (a second peak of 70,000 cfs followed on 11 April) while 2025 was a single sharp spike. Downstream at Imboden the 2025 flood was clearly the larger — 128,000 cfs against 97,300, the second-largest there since 1937 behind only December 1982 — which says the 2025 storm fell more heavily on the lower basin between the two gauges. The 2025 peak is still provisional and may be revised slightly.

When they come, and whether they cluster

Floods here are a late-winter event. The average date of Imboden's yearly peak is 24 February across 89 years, and the concentration around that date is the same in the 1940s as in the 2010s. The gaps between major (16 ft+) floods at Hardy look like a coin-flip process — a big flood does not make the next one more or less likely (7 events; the spread of gaps is what a memoryless process produces, bootstrap p = 0.47). The idea that a major flood is followed by a quiet year has no support either: it happened 0 of 5 times.

Floods and the spring

Does a big flood drain the spring afterward? no — the opposite

A common worry is that a flood flushes the aquifer and leaves the spring low for months. To test it we took the six floods of 16 feet or more, waited 30 days for the flood to recede, and compared the following six months of base flow with the same months in three non-flood years matched on rain and on how full the spring was beforehand.

Two high summers

Why 2020 and 2025 ran so high

The two highest summers in the record are recent — June–August 2025 (spring 488 cfs, Hardy 1,379 cfs) and 2020 (483 and 1,200) — and the reason is the same mechanism that makes dry years dry, run the other way. High summers are made in the winter and spring before, and doubly so when the year before that was wet too.

  • 2020 sat on two wet years stacked. The recharge area received 61.6 in in the twelve months to August 2019 and 59.4 in in the twelve to August 2020 (normal is about 47) — the second-wettest two-year stretch in 45 years. October–November 2019, January–March 2020 and May 2020 all ran well above normal, and the summer itself kept pace. The reservoir underneath was full, so the spring held near 500 cfs into July.
  • 2025 got there in one year. The twelve months to August 2025 brought 62.1 in — the wettest twelve-month window in the record — after a merely average year. The April 2025 flood and a wet spring drove Hardy to 2,800 cfs in May and 2,400 in June; then, with less water stored underneath and a drier summer (18 in May–August against 22 in 2020), both gauges fell faster and by August the two years had converged.
Rain

Not more rain — but more one-inch days, and perhaps harder ones intensity

Yearly rainfall over the recharge area shows no clear trend since 1981: +0.96 inches a decade with an interval from −1.3 to +3.1, which includes zero. The same is true on the second gridded dataset.

What the gridded data show instead is intensity. Since 1981 the wettest single day of the year has grown by about a quarter-inch a decade; the average rain on a rainy day has grown; the number of one-inch-plus days has grown by about 1.4 a decade. The winter recharge season — September to February, when rain actually reaches the aquifer — shows no trend. So over the last 45 years: the same water, arriving in harder doses, and not in the months that matter most for the spring.

The West Plains gauge, read over its full 1949–2025 record, agrees only in part. It shows more one-inch days (+0.7 a decade, interval +0.3 to +1.2, 76 years) and a yearly total drifting up (+1.3 in a decade, interval +0.3 to +2.3) that does not survive correction for testing ten things at once. It shows no trend in the wettest day, the wettest three days, or rain per rainy day. Two honest readings of that: the intensification is a feature of the last 45 years that a 77-year record dilutes, or the grid's intensity signal is stronger than what a single gauge can see. The gauge is one point; the grid is an area over a shorter period. Neither settles it.

Rain to spring

How long rain takes to reach the spring

Comparing month-by-month rainfall anomalies with month-by-month spring flow, the strongest match is with a one-month delay, and the relationship is gone after about eight months. A wet March shows up at the spring in April. The result is the same on both rainfall datasets.

This year

Is 2026 unusual? the spring rise never came

The rain that recharges the aquifer falls on West Plains and the country south-east of it — the town's gauges sit inside the traced recharge area, about four kilometres in from its north-west edge — and West Plains has been measuring rain daily since 1948. That is the longest rain record we have for this water, so this section ranks 2026 against the whole of it, not just the 45 years the gridded rainfall covers.

Two gauges make up that record. The cooperative gauge in town (a volunteer reading a rain can each morning since 1948) has hundreds of unread days in 2011–2021; the airport's automated gauge, 10.7 miles north of it and 120 feet higher, has recorded almost every day since April 1998. The record below is the cooperative gauge through March 1998 and the airport gauge from April 1998 on — one instrument per period, no day borrowed from the other. Because the two gauges do not catch the same amount (see below), the airport readings are raised by the measured difference so the whole record sits on the town gauge's level.

Through 23 August 2026 that record shows 34.6 in since the water year began on 1 October — the 14th-driest such stretch in 77 years (median 40.5). The last 365 days total 41.0 in, 25th-driest (median 45.4): a middling year. The September-to-February recharge season, when rain actually reaches the aquifer instead of being used by plants, delivered 13.6 in — the 10th-driest recharge season since 1949 (median 20.8; 1977 at 8.8 and 2017 at 10.7 were far drier). December brought 0.4 in against a normal 3.8; January and February each about a third of normal. Spring was near normal and June was wet, which is why the year's total looks ordinary while the spring's reserve is thin.

How different are the two gauges? Over their 282 overlapping months the town gauge catches 6.8 % more than the airport — on average about 4 in a year, though the gap swings from −3 to +14 in from one year to the next. The record here multiplies the airport readings by that 1.068 so the two halves match. Unadjusted, this year's numbers are 32.4 in (water year to date), 38.4 in (last 365 days) and 12.8 in (recharge season), and the town gauge's own readings for the same windows — with 13 unread days — are 33.9, 37.5 and 12.4. Whichever way it is counted, the recharge season ranks between 8th and 10th driest since 1949.

And the river?

Two different things are true. The floor — the driest week of the year — is unremarkable: 231 cfs at Mammoth Spring (21st-lowest of 46, a median year at the source) and 314 cfs at Hardy (7th of 25, well above the 2002–2007 run of 191–282). Put this year's rain into the base-flow model from the section above and it predicts about 335 cfs at Hardy and 213 at Mammoth; the observed values are within the model's ordinary scatter.

What was genuinely unusual is the season. In most years the river rises through March, April and May on winter recharge and spring storms; in 2026 it never did. March–June averaged 560 cfs at Hardy — the lowest of the 24 years on record, against a typical 1,255 — and 291 cfs at Mammoth Spring, the 4th-lowest of 46 (only 1981, 2000 and 2001 were lower). Three-quarters of those days sat below the 10th percentile for their date at both gauges. Summer then arrived at a level the river usually reaches only in September. That is the consequence of one of the ten driest recharge seasons since 1948 followed by a spring without a single large storm — not a change in how the spring behaves, but a year that skipped its high season.

If the Hardy gauge looks like it has never been lower, that is the stage reading. The channel at the gauge has scoured about a quarter of a foot since 2012, so the stage sets new lows the flow does not (see "Why the stage readings look lower").

Limits

What this page cannot tell you

  • Hardy's record is short. 24 years for low flows and floods; anything at Hardy beyond a 20-year return period is extrapolation. Mammoth Spring (45 years) and Imboden (89 years of peaks) carry the long conclusions.
  • The gauge's datum history is unverified. The USGS's direct discharge measurements have been checked against the rating and agree; the formal datum and shift records have not been reviewed, so the stage figures carry that residual uncertainty.
  • The rain is a model product. AORC blends radar, gauges and reanalysis into a grid, and before 2002 it has no radar at all. The two rain grids are not independent. The West Plains and Alton gauges have too many missing years (Alton: 1983–94 and 2012–16) to confirm or deny the trends on their own.
  • The rain record stops at 31 December 2025. NOAA publishes AORC in whole calendar years, months after the fact. One day (18 June 2024) is missing from the source and is left blank rather than filled.
  • The traced area is the spring's only. Water shared with the neighbouring Bill Mac and Greer springs is excluded by the boundary we used.
  • Six floods make a pattern, not a proof. The post-flood finding is a comparison, not an experiment, and its interval does not include the uncertainty in how control years were chosen.
  • Provisional data. Discharge after April 2026 is provisional. Every result was rerun on approved data only; none changed.
Reading the numbers

Three things to know about the figures on this page

The interval in parentheses
Every trend is given with a 95 % interval — the range of values the data are consistent with. If it includes zero, the data cannot tell a rise from a fall from nothing. "Not significant" means exactly that, not "there is no trend".
n
How many years, events, or months the number rests on. Six is a pattern; forty-five is a record; ninety is a long record.
"Unsettled"
A result that depends on which rainfall dataset is used, or rests on too few years to call. We report it, we do not conclude from it.
