Spring River in Plain Terms

What forty-five years of gauges and rainfall say about a spring-fed Ozark river — and what changed when we drew the watershed correctly.

Gauges Mammoth Spring (the source), Hardy (the town), Imboden (downstream) Rain NOAA AORC hourly grid over the traced recharge area; West Plains gauge 1948–2026 Last data discharge 24 Aug 2026 · rain 31 Dec 2025

The Spring River does not behave like most rivers. Its base flow — the water that is there when it has not rained — comes out of the ground at Mammoth Spring, one of the largest springs in the Ozarks, fed by an underground drainage area that straddles the Missouri line. On top of that steady floor sit short, violent floods that arrive with heavy rain and leave within days.

People who live on, paddle, or fish the river have asked the same questions for years: is the floor sinking? Are floods getting bigger or more frequent? Is it raining more? Does a big flood wash out the spring's supply? This page answers each one from the numbers, says how sure we are, and is honest about the two answers that changed in this edition.

Where the water comes from

The watershed we should have been measuring

Rain that feeds Mammoth Spring does not fall on the river. It falls on a plateau of fractured limestone to the north-east, sinks, and travels underground to the spring. The first edition of this study approximated that area as a 30-kilometre circle around West Plains, Missouri, because no traced boundary was in hand.

This edition uses the Missouri Geological Survey's dye-traced recharge area instead. It is about 349 square miles — roughly a third of the circle — and sits south-east of West Plains toward Alton and Thayer, not centred on the town. Rainfall is now taken from NOAA's AORC analysis, a one-kilometre hourly grid that blends radar (from 2002), rain gauges, and weather-model output, averaged over that traced area.

Base flow

Is the spring's floor sinking? holds — no

Each year we take the driest seven consecutive days at Mammoth Spring and ask what explains them. The answer is last year's rain. The lowest week tracks how much fell over the twelve months before it, with a one-year echo, and once that is removed the leftover trend is −0.13 % a year with a range that comfortably includes zero (95 % interval −0.33 % to +0.12 %, 42 years). Drawing the watershed correctly made rain a better predictor — it now explains 58 % of the year-to-year swing, up from 45 % — and left the "no decline" verdict where it was.

At Hardy the floor is rising changed — unsettled

Hardy's own record only starts in 2002. Its driest week has climbed from around 200 cfs to around 400 cfs over those 24 years, and that part is not in doubt (+2.3 % a year, interval +0.7 to +3.6). The question is why. Antecedent rain explains part of it. What is left over depends on which rainfall dataset you use:

  • On AORC, the primary dataset of this edition, the unexplained rise is +2.0 % a year and its interval (+0.9 to +2.9) does not include zero.
  • On PRISM over the same traced area, it is +1.0 % a year with an interval that touches zero (−0.06 to +1.9).
  • On the old circle, +0.7 % a year, interval −0.1 to +2.0.

Two of three say "nothing beyond rain"; the one we trust most says "something beyond rain". With 24 years and a model that explains only 41 % of the variation, we report this as source-dependent rather than as a finding. It is the first thing the next review should attack.

The gauge

Why the stage readings look lower holds

Anyone watching the Hardy gauge has seen the late-summer stage sit lower each year — 3.35 feet in 2015, 3.0 in 2017, under 3 feet now. But stage is a ruler on a riverbed, and the riverbed moves. Comparing readings at the same flow, the stage at 1,000 cfs has fallen 0.019 ft a year (interval 0.016 to 0.021, 19 years) — about a quarter of a foot since 2012. The channel at the gauge has scoured down; the water is not gone. The USGS keeps records of when it re-rated this gauge, and we have not obtained them yet, so this rests on the readings alone.

Floods

How often, how big, and is it changing? holds — no change

Hardy has only 24 annual peaks, so for trend we lean on Imboden, 30 miles downstream, which has 89 (1937–2025). Imboden's yearly peaks show no drift (+0.1 % a year, interval −0.3 to +0.9). Splitting the record before and after 2008 — the year of the biggest modern flood — gives ten-year flood estimates whose uncertainty ranges overlap. Nothing in the data says floods are becoming larger or more common.

When they come, and whether they cluster

Floods here are a late-winter event. The average date of Imboden's yearly peak is 24 February across 89 years, and the concentration around that date is the same in the 1940s as in the 2010s. The gaps between major (16 ft+) floods at Hardy look like a coin-flip process — a big flood does not make the next one more or less likely (7 events; the spread of gaps is what a memoryless process produces, bootstrap p = 0.47). The idea that a major flood is followed by a quiet year has no support either: it happened 0 of 5 times.

Floods and the spring

Does a big flood drain the spring afterward? holds — no, the opposite

A common worry is that a flood flushes the aquifer and leaves the spring low for months. To test it we took the six floods of 16 feet or more, waited 30 days for the flood to recede, and compared the following six months of base flow with the same months in three non-flood years matched on rain and on how full the spring was beforehand.

Rain

More intense, not more of it changed

The first edition reported that yearly rainfall over the basin was rising by about 2.4 inches a decade, and that the rise was statistically solid. Over the traced recharge area it is not: +0.96 inches a decade with an interval from −1.3 to +3.1. The earlier number was a property of the oversized circle, which reached into wetter ground to the north-west.

What does hold, on every dataset, is intensity. The wettest single day of the year has grown by about a quarter-inch a decade; the average rain on a rainy day has grown; the number of one-inch-plus days has grown by about 1.4 a decade. The winter recharge season — September to February, when rain actually reaches the aquifer — shows no trend at all. So: the same water, arriving in harder doses, and not in the months that matter most for the spring.

Rain to spring

How long rain takes to reach the spring holds

Comparing month-by-month rainfall anomalies with month-by-month spring flow, the strongest match is with a one-month delay, and the relationship is gone after about eight months. A wet March shows up at the spring in April. The result was the same on all three rainfall datasets.

This year

Is 2026 unusual? dry winter — yes; dry year — no

The rain that recharges the aquifer falls on West Plains and the country south-east of it — the gauge sits inside the traced recharge area, about four kilometres in from its north-west edge — and the West Plains cooperative gauge has been read daily since 1948. That is the longest rain record we have for this water, so this section ranks 2026 against all 78 years of it, not just the 45 the study's grids cover.

Through 23 August 2026 the gauge has recorded 33.9 in since the water year began on 1 October — the 11th-driest such stretch in 67 usable years (median 40.0). The last 365 days total 37.5 in, 13th-driest (median 45.2). Dry, but a dozen years since 1949 were drier, and 1953 was drier by a wide margin.

What is rare is the timing. The September-to-February recharge season, when rain actually reaches the aquifer instead of being used by plants, delivered 12.4 in — the fourth-driest recharge season since 1949 (median 20.6; only 1977, 1954 and 1964 were drier). December brought 0.4 in against a normal 3.8; January and February each about a third of normal. Spring was near normal and June was wet (6.4 in against 3.5), which is why the year's total looks ordinary while the spring's reserve is thin.

And the river?

Mammoth Spring's driest week so far this year (231 cfs, late May) ranks 21st of 46 — a median year at the source. Hardy's driest week (314 cfs, mid-August) ranks 7th of 25, well above the 2002–2007 run (191–282 cfs), though the water year has five weeks left and Hardy is still falling. Put this year's rain into the study's attribution model and it predicts a low week of about 335 cfs at Hardy and 213 at Mammoth; the observed 314 and 231 are within the model's ordinary scatter. In other words, 2026's low water is what a fourth-driest winter produces — not a sign of the spring changing.

If the Hardy gauge looks like it has never been lower, that is the stage reading. The channel at the gauge has scoured about a quarter of a foot since 2012, so the stage sets new lows the flow does not (see "Why the stage readings look lower").

What changed when we drew the watershed correctly

Limits

What this page cannot tell you

  • Hardy's record is short. 24 years for low flows and floods; anything at Hardy beyond a 20-year return period is extrapolation. Mammoth Spring (45 years) and Imboden (89 years of peaks) carry the long conclusions.
  • The gauge's own history is missing. The channel-scour finding comes from readings alone; the USGS re-rating and datum records have not been obtained.
  • The rain is a model product. AORC blends radar, gauges and reanalysis into a grid, and before 2002 it has no radar at all. The two rain grids are not independent. The West Plains and Alton gauges have too many missing years (Alton: 1983–94 and 2012–16) to confirm or deny the trends on their own.
  • The rain record stops at 31 December 2025. NOAA publishes AORC in whole calendar years, months after the fact. One day (18 June 2024) is missing from the source and is left blank rather than filled.
  • The traced area is the spring's only. Water shared with the neighbouring Bill Mac and Greer springs is excluded by the boundary we used.
  • Six floods make a pattern, not a proof. The post-flood finding is a comparison, not an experiment, and its interval does not include the uncertainty in how control years were chosen.
  • Provisional data. Discharge after April 2026 is provisional. Every result was rerun on approved data only; none changed.
Reading the numbers

Three things to know about the figures on this page

The interval in parentheses
Every trend is given with a 95 % interval — the range of values the data are consistent with. If it includes zero, the data cannot tell a rise from a fall from nothing. "Not significant" means exactly that, not "there is no trend".
n
How many years, events, or months the number rests on. Six is a pattern; forty-five is a record; ninety is a long record.
"Holds" and "changed"
"Holds" means the first edition's conclusion survived the corrected watershed and the new rainfall data. "Changed" means it did not, and the text explains what replaced it.
